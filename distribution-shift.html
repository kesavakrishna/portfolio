What distribution shift looks like in a production RAG system

July 2026 · ≈6 min · RADEX, Bilvantis Technologies

One of the systems I built at Bilvantis was RADEX, a multi-tenant conversational AI platform: enterprise clients connect their document stores and databases, and their teams ask questions in plain language. Under the hood — partitioned Milvus vector stores with tenant isolation, hybrid retrieval over unstructured documents, and a text-to-SQL agent for the structured side. At launch it worked. Evaluations looked strong, pilot clients were happy, the demo rarely missed.

Then, over the months that followed, it got worse — slowly, unevenly, and without a single error in the logs. This is a post-mortem of that degradation: what silent failure looks like in a RAG system, how we found it, and why it changed what I want to work on.

01Nothing crashed

The first thing to understand about RAG failures is that the infrastructure has no opinion about quality. Vector search always returns the top-k nearest neighbors — but nearest is not relevant, and cosine similarity has no threshold at which it admits defeat. The language model then does what language models do: writes a fluent, confident answer from whatever context it was handed. Every component reports success. The answer is simply worse.

So the system didn't fail the way software usually fails. Latency was normal. Uptime was fine. Retries, token counts, queue depths — all green. What we had instead was a slow leak of trust: users on certain tenants describing answers as "off" or "generic" — the kind of feedback that arrives through account managers, not alerting systems.

The only metric that was truly failing was one we weren't measuring.

02Where the shift came from

"Distribution shift" sounds like one thing. In a multi-tenant RAG system it is at least four, and they compound:

  • New tenants aren't your pilot tenants. Every retrieval decision — chunk sizes, hybrid weighting, prompt scaffolding — had been tuned, explicitly or implicitly, against the early clients' documents and the early users' questions. Each new tenant brought new vocabulary, new document formats, a new mix of question types. The system they got was optimized for someone else.
  • Corpora drift under you. A knowledge base is not a dataset; it's a stream. Documents added months after launch didn't look like the documents the chunking strategy was designed around, and the embedding space quietly filled with material the original design never saw.
  • Users adapt. Early users ask cautious, keyword-shaped questions. Once they trust the system, they ask real ones: compound, conversational, full of internal shorthand. The queries evolve precisely because the system worked at first.
  • The routing boundary moves. RADEX answered from documents or from SQL, and a router chose which. As the mix of incoming questions shifted, routing decisions tuned on the old mix began to misfire on the new one — failures that look like retrieval problems but aren't.

The common thread: we had optimized against a snapshot, and production is a stream.

03Diagnosing what you can't grep for

There is no stack trace for "the answers got worse." Looking at outputs told us little — a bad answer doesn't say why it's bad. The signal was upstream, in the retrieval layer: how similarity scores distributed per tenant and how those distributions moved over time; how much retrieved sets churned for near-identical queries; how often the router sent questions across the document/SQL boundary.

And beneath all the aggregates, the most useful practice was the least glamorous one: reading transcripts. One conversation at a time, tenant by tenant, until the pattern showed itself. The diagnosis, once we could state it plainly, was simple: the queries the system was receiving no longer looked like the queries it had been tuned and evaluated on — and they had drifted differently for each tenant.

04What actually fixed it

The fixes were less dramatic than the diagnosis, and the durable ones were as much process as code:

  • Per-tenant evaluation sets, refreshed from real (anonymized) production queries and run like regression tests — so quality became a number that could go red before a client said anything.
  • Monitoring the input distribution, not just system health. If this month's queries don't resemble last month's, that is an alert — even when every service is up.
  • Treating retrieval quality as a first-class production metric, with the same seriousness as latency or uptime.
  • Targeted changes over global re-tuning — adjusting chunking and routing where the evidence pointed, tenant by tenant, rather than reshuffling the whole system on instinct.

None of this is research-grade novelty — which is partly the point. The engineering discipline that catches distribution shift is ordinary. What's missing, mostly, is the habit of treating deployment as the start of evaluation rather than the end of it.

05Why this sent me back to school

A benchmark is a photograph; production is weather. The gap between how models evaluate and how they behave months into deployment is, I think, one of the most consequential and understudied parts of the stack. I watched that gap open in a real system with real users — and the tools I had for understanding why behavior changed were embarrassingly blunt: score distributions and transcripts. Statistics and squinting.

That's the itch behind my next step. In Fall 2026 I'm starting an MS in Data Science at CU Boulder, aiming at research on reliability under distribution shift and on interpretability — because "the answers got worse" should be a measurable, explainable, attributable event, not a vibe reported by account managers.

The waveform on my homepage starts as noise and resolves into a clean signal. The honest version of that story is that in production, the resolving is never finished. The noise always comes back. The systems worth building are the ones that notice.
